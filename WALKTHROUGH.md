# The whole project in plain English

**Purpose of this file:** a walkthrough anyone could follow — you in three weeks,
a supervisor, a friend who knows programming but not networking. No jargon without
a definition (see the glossary at the end). Read top to bottom; it goes from "what
is this thesis about" to "what exists right now and what is broken".

Provenance tags where they matter: **[V]** = verified in a full text we have,
**[S]** = seen only at abstract/metadata level, **[U]** = our judgement, not yet
verified against a source.

---

## 1. The one-paragraph version

A Software-Defined Network splits the network into a **brain** (the controller,
which decides how packets should be handled) and **muscle** (switches, which just
obey the brain). Real networks use *several* brains, each owning part of the
network, because one brain cannot scale or survive failure. That creates a new
problem: some brains get much more work than others. This thesis is about the
brains of a **vehicular** network — a city or highway where the switches
effectively move at 50 km/h and cross between brain-domains constantly, so the
workload is generated by movement itself. The thesis builds a learning agent
(deep reinforcement learning, specifically a **DQN** whose memory is an
**LSTM**) that watches load history and moves switches between controllers to keep
the work evenly spread — *before* a controller drowns, not after. Its claim to
novelty is that prior work either has no memory in the decision-maker, or has no
vehicles. It aims to have neither of those holes. [V/U]

## 2. The world the thesis lives in

**2.1 SDN in one minute.** In a normal router, every packet's fate is decided
inside that box by rules the vendor baked in. SDN pulls that decision logic out
into ordinary software — a *controller* — and leaves the boxes as simple
executors of a rule table. The controller and the switches talk over a
"southbound" protocol; OpenFlow is the standard one. The payoff is that network
behaviour becomes a program you can test, version and scale, which is why this is
a Software Engineering thesis and not a networking one. [V]

**2.2 What a controller actually does all day.** When a switch receives a packet
that matches no rule, it wraps the header in a message ("**packet-in**") and asks
the controller what to do. The controller answers ("**flow-mod**" — install this
rule). So a controller's work is *answering questions*, not moving bytes. Its CPU
is burned by new flows, telemetry, topology events, and the answers it writes
back. Tootoonchian & Ganjali (HOT-ICE 2012) showed a single controller saturates
at a fairly modest packet-in rate — that saturation is the thing this thesis
tries to avoid locally, by spreading the questions out. [V/S]

**2.3 Why the network has several brains.** One controller means one point of
failure, unbounded delay to distant switches, and a hard CPU ceiling. Splitting
the network into domains, each with a controller, fixes those three things and
introduces two new problems: which switches belong to which controller (the
*Controller Placement Problem*), and keeping the controllers consistent with each
other (*East-West* traffic). Across the survey literature, the consensus is that
distributed control doesn't just inherit imbalance — it **creates load imbalance
as a first-class failure mode**. That failure mode is this thesis's reason to
exist. [S/U]

**2.4 What vehicles change — the important asymmetry.** In a data centre, the
switches stay put and the workload moves around them. In a vehicular network it
is the opposite: the *endpoints* move, and the controller domains (a city centre,
a highway corridor) are static. So when a platoon of cars crosses a domain border,
one controller suddenly receives a burst of new registrations plus a burst of
packet-ins, while its neighbour goes quiet. The hotspot is *manufactured by
movement*, and it happens on a timescale of seconds — comparable to how long a
controller's queue takes to recover. That is why the load process here is not
stationary and cannot be read off the current instant: you have to watch the
history. This is the technical heart of the whole thesis. [V/U]

## 3. The actual problem being solved

**3.1 What "load" means here.** A controller's load is its **service queue** —
the backlog of questions waiting to be answered — not how much traffic flows
through the network. The field measures it as request rate, CPU utilisation,
flow-setup latency, or flow-table occupancy; the common default is *request rate*
as a proxy for CPU. Our environment models it as an M/M/1-style queue with a
service rate μ (questions answered per tick) and a finite capacity; a growing
queue means rising response latency, and past the capacity it means dropped
control messages, which to a user looks like a stalled connection. [V: LR-20]

**3.2 How imbalance forms.** Dynamic traffic means some domains' switches
generate more first-packets than others → queues grow unevenly → latency rises →
beyond capacity, control messages are dropped. In SDVN, the same thing happens
spatially: vehicle density shifts with rush hours and platoons. [V/U]

**3.3 The actuator — what the agent is allowed to do.** The intervention ladder,
cheapest to most expensive: (i) **switch migration** / re-association — move a
switch's control relationship from one controller to another; (ii) master/equal/
slave *role* adjustment; (iii) spinning controllers up and down; (iv) *placement* —
deciding where controllers live at all (an offline planning problem). This thesis
takes the first rung: migration/roles, because it is fast and fine-grained.
Placement is adjacent work, explicitly not ours. [V]

**3.4 Why acting costs something — and why thrashing is the real enemy.** Moving
a switch is not free: session state must be transferred or rebuilt, the two
controllers must synchronise, and there is a brief window of uncertainty. A naive
balancer therefore oscillates — move a switch, the imbalance flips, move it back —
paying migration cost forever. The classic insight (from the EASM paper) is that
optimising *pure balance* is wrong; you must optimise balance **against** migration
cost. That insight is why our reward has a migration-cost term and a
re-migration ("you moved this thing twice in a row") penalty. [V]

## 4. What the approved proposal commits us to

This matters because the proposal is a contract with the committee; the thesis may
improve on it but not silently contradict it. From the approved v05 document
(18 Apr 2025):

- **Official title:** *Load-Balancing in Distributed SDVN controllers with
  DQN+LSTM*.
- **Approved tooling:** Python, **Mininet-WiFi + Ryu**, with "Emulation vs
  Simulation" named as the implementation novelty; NGSIM/HighD traces "if
  available".
- **Named comparison target:** Marwein et al. 2024 (*Computer Networks*
  254:110805) — "how much improvement versus the reference scheme?" is literally
  research question #2 of the proposal. That makes Marwein's approach a
  **mandatory baseline**, not an optional one.
- **Its variable set:** controller CPU, controller throughput, handover frequency
  and delay, network latency, vehicle density, end-to-end latency, packet loss,
  plus learning parameters.
- **Its formulas:** a fairness-ish index E_lb = 1 − σ(C_cpu)/μ(C_cpu) (i.e. one
  minus the coefficient of variation), a QoS score 1/(Le2e + Ploss + H_delay), a
  controller efficiency η, and a reward R = ω₁η + ω₂QoS − ω₃·overhead. A vanilla
  DQN update rule is written out.
- **Its hypotheses:** H1 the scheme reduces *switch handover* versus heuristic
  schemes; H2 adaptivity improves balance and capacity; H3 tuning the learning
  rate and LSTM time-steps materially helps; H4 it stays stable under heavy,
  dynamic traffic.
- **Its research questions:** RQ1 how to combine DQN and LSTM for vehicular load
  balancing; RQ2 how big the improvement is versus the reference scheme and which
  parameters matter most; RQ3 how it behaves in real-time/dynamic traffic.
- **Its timeline:** 12 months from Farvardin 1404 (≈ March 2025). We are past the
  band the proposal itself drew; the plan already accounts for acceleration.

**Known weaknesses of the approved document** (to be fixed in the thesis prose,
never by editing the approved form): DQN is mislabelled as generic "deep neural
network"; **vehicle handover is conflated with controller migration** — two
different kinds of churn that the thesis must separate explicitly (examiners will
notice); the E_lb formula can go negative and can reward a low-average, high-noise
regime; the reward mixes unnormalised units, so the weights need a documented
tuning protocol; the reference list is thin (6 sources) for a defence. [V: LR-05]

## 5. The one genuine ambiguity in the approved text — and how it was resolved

The title says "DQN+LSTM" and the approved methodology reads it as a **pipeline**:
LSTM predicts future traffic, then the DQN uses those predictions to choose a
policy. But the literature we read early on pointed at the other reading: the LSTM
*inside* the DQN as the state encoder — a recurrent Q-network (the DRQN family).
Two different architectures wearing the same four-word label.

Resolution adopted (**O1, no amendment needed**): implement the pipeline **as
written** — it is the committed system — *and* run the recurrent-encoder variant as
the thesis's internal architecture comparison, which is a perfectly legitimate
answer to the proposal's own RQ2 ("which parameters/architecture matter most").
There is published precedent for the pipeline in general SDN (Xiao et al. 2024, [V]),
and the variant is cheap on our fast environment. So the ambiguity becomes an
experiment rather than a problem: pipeline vs recurrent encoder vs stacked-window
frames. [V: LR-05 §3a]

## 6. What the literature actually says — and the gap we now claim

The review was done in three passes, each one narrowing the other:

1. **Local library:** 24 PDFs already on disk were audited and indexed (`papers-index.md`).
2. **36-month harvest (2023-09 → 2026-09):** a sweep across OpenAlex, IEEE Xplore's
   API, Crossref, arXiv and S2 produced **2,617 unique in-window papers**. From
   those, a curated shortlist (T1/T2) of **30 papers** that carry a load-balancing
   or controller-placement signal. [S]
3. **Full-text pass:** **22 of the 30** were pulled in full and machine-extracted
   (text + figures) into `~/research/thesis-search-2026-09/library/t1/`, then
   probed by term counts. [V]

**What the 30-paper matrix shows.** Tallied across the 30: recurrent models in
10, reinforcement learning in 13, prediction in 9, vehicular context in 7,
mobility in 4, fractional actions in 3. **Recurrent ∧ RL ∧ vehicular = 0.** [S]

**What the full texts add — the strongest single fact in the thesis so far.**
Searching all 22 full texts for "recurrent Q / DRQN / recurrent DQN / R2D2" gives
**zero hits in every single paper**, while LSTM/GRU appears in 10 of them. In other
words: recurrence in this literature is used *exclusively to forecast* load, never
as the decision-maker's memory. POMDP language appears in exactly one paper;
Jain's index in only three; no paper reports the mobility columns we intend to
report. [V]

**The same story in plain words.** The field has settled on one recipe — "predict
the load, then move switches" — and the prediction step is where the memory lives.
The decision step is memoryless: it looks at the current instant and reacts. And
almost nobody does any of this in a network where the load is *created by cars
moving*. So there are two holes side by side, and nobody has stood in both.

**Gap statement v3 (proposal-grade).** Load balancing in the SDN control plane is
now routinely predictive and increasingly fractional, but this literature is
almost entirely non-vehicular and treats the policy as memoryless; in vehicular
SDN, reinforcement learning tackles controller selection/placement while mobility
is handled separately as handover management. **The unclaimed intersection is a
recurrent (LSTM-encoded) DQN performing control-plane load balancing in a
distributed SDVN, evaluated under an explicit mobility model.** [V/U]

**Papers we must cite and explicitly contrast** (they are the nearest neighbours,
so an examiner will ask): the 6G SDN vehicular MARL load balancer; the BiLSTM
distributed-SDN load balancer; the recurrent controller-load predictors; and the
recurrent-DQN controller-placement paper. Each of them fails at least two of our
axes. [S]

## 7. What the method looks like

**7.1 The decision problem, in plain words.** Each controller in a distributed
control plane only really knows its own state — its own queue, its own arrivals.
Reports from neighbouring controllers may arrive late, or not at all. So the agent
is a manager who can see their own branch's books clearly and everyone else's only
through delayed, occasionally missing memos. This is formally a **POMDP** (a
decision problem where you don't see the true state, only a partial, noisy view of
it), and it is ours *by construction*, not by accident: our environment never hands
the agent the global load vector. That is deliberate — it is the reason a memory is
useful rather than decorative. If the agent could see everything instantly, the
LSTM would be decoration. [U]

**7.2 What the agent sees.** A sliding window of the last 8 ticks, per controller:
queue length, a smoothed arrival rate, service rate, an overload flag, how many
vehicles are in the zone, the incoming handover rate, and how long since this
controller last lost a switch. Neighbour blocks are sometimes zeroed out and
flagged in a mask vector — that's the "missing memo" simulated, at dropout rates
0 %, 15 % and 30 %.

**7.3 What the agent does.** Discrete actions: do nothing, or move one of the 3
most-loaded switches of the most-loaded controller to one of the other
controllers. With 3 controllers that's 7 actions. A "move a fraction of the
switch's load" variant exists in the literature and is allowed, but is kept as an
ablation so that our agent stays comparable with the baselines.

**7.4 What it is rewarded for.** Each tick, a penalty made of four parts: unfairness
across controllers (Jain's index), migration cost, the number of controllers
currently overloaded, and re-migration (punishment for flapping). Weights are
config-tunable and must be reported as the single scalarisation used for every
agent — comparing agents under different rewards would be meaningless.

**7.5 How success is measured.** Fairness (Jain's index over controller load) and
p95 packet-in response latency as primaries; migrations per slot and cumulative
migration cost, re-migration rate and overload-event count as guard-rails; plus two
columns no prior paper reports — **lead time** (how many seconds *before* an
overload event the agent acted, which is exactly what falsifies the "proactive"
claim against reactive baselines) and **handover-induced migration rate**. We
report a superset of the field's metrics precisely because the field reports
disjoint subsets.

**7.6 Two tiers of environment.** Tier 1 is a fast abstract simulator in Python/NumPy
(a tick loop with controllers, queues, vehicles, handovers and a migration
actuator) — fast enough to train an RL agent on, which a full network emulator is
not. Tier 2 is **Mininet-WiFi + Ryu** with real 802.11p emulation, where one
trained policy is replayed to show the results point the same way. Tier 2 is the
chapter the proposal promised ("Emulation vs Simulation"); tier 1 is what makes
training feasible. The decision to split it this way is recorded in
`02-methodology/env-decisions/2026-08-31-abstract-env-vs-mininet.md`.

**7.7 The baselines.** We compare against: B0 never migrate (the floor); B1 greedy
least-loaded, cost-blind; B2 threshold-triggered migration with a cooldown (the
classical reactive family); B3 random legal migration (a sanity control); B4 a
memoryless DQN on stacked frames (the honest rival that would kill the memory
claim if it ties); B5 **ours** — Double/Dueling DQN with an LSTM encoder; B6
Marwein-style hierarchical partition (mandated by the proposal). B0–B3 exist as
code today; B4–B6 do not.

## 9. Where we are actually stuck (and why that's a good place to be stuck)

The environment runs, but its numbers don't yet mean anything, for three different
kinds of reason:

1. **Measurement bugs.** The reward is never accumulated by the smoke harness (it
   reports 0.0 for every policy), so the reward function is *unverified*. Service
   accounting is inflated by being counted before the queue is clipped. Packet drops
   are taken *before* service in the same tick, so a full controller drops messages it
   had capacity to answer. Total handover count is an exponentially-smoothed value, not
   a count.
2. **The operating regime is wrong.** Every policy loses about 23 % of control
   messages and p95 latency is pinned at 3.0 against a hard ceiling of 4.0 — including
   the policy that never migrates. When the do-nothing lower bound and the well-balanced
   policy lose almost the same fraction, the loss is not coming from *imbalance*, which
   is the phenomenon the thesis claims to fix. The environment is currently drowning
   rather than being lop-sided.
3. **A metric that can be gamed.** The threshold policy scores Jain 0.995 — near-perfect
   fairness — while dropping 23 % of packets. Jain's index over queue length treats
   "everyone equally underwater" as perfectly fair. So the primary metric currently
   rewards exactly the outcome the thesis is supposed to prevent. This has to be settled
   before any agent is trained, because a learned policy will happily optimise the
   artefact we leave in.

None of this invalidates the design; it means the instrument isn't calibrated yet.
The fix list is the "item 1" plan: repair the measurement bugs, diagnose where the
loss actually comes from (per-controller drop timeline), settle the metric definition,
then calibrate the demand/capacity regime with a sweep, then freeze and write the
amendment. Only after that does B4/B5 (the agent) become worth building — in
particular the LSTM-versus-stacked-frames comparison, which is the experiment that
carries the entire novelty claim and which a broken fairness metric would flatten.

## 11. Glossary

- **SDN** — a network where the decision logic (control plane) is separated from the
  forwarding devices (data plane).
- **Controller** — the software brain of an SDN; answers switches' questions.
- **SDVN** — SDN applied to vehicular networks; endpoints move, RSUs are roadside
  base stations.
- **RSU** — roadside unit; in our model it plays the role of a "switch" that belongs
  to a controller.
- **Control plane / data plane** — decisions / execution.
- **Packet-in** — a switch asking the controller what to do with an unmatched packet;
  the unit of controller workload.
- **Switch migration** — moving a switch's control relationship from one controller to
  another; our agent's action.
- **Handover** — a *vehicle* changing which RSU serves it. Different from migration;
  the approved proposal conflates them and the thesis must not.
- **Jain's fairness index** — a 0-to-1 score of how evenly a quantity is distributed
  across parties; 1 means perfectly even. Note the caveat in §9.3: even can also mean
  equally bad.
- **POMDP** — a decision problem where the true state is only partially observed.
- **DQN** — Deep Q-Network: a value-based reinforcement-learning method that learns how
  good each discrete action is, using a neural network. Fits "which switch moves where".
- **LSTM / GRU** — recurrent neural networks with memory over time; used here to read a
  window of history.
- **DRQN** — a DQN whose state encoder is recurrent (our recurrent variant); part of the
  lineage of this thesis's agent.
- **Double / Dueling DQN** — standard stabilising refinements to DQN, used as ablation
  axes.
- **Lead time** — how early before an overload the agent acts; our novel proactive metric.
- **Tier 1 / Tier 2** — the fast training simulator and the Mininet-WiFi + Ryu
  validation emulation.
- **B0 … B6** — the baselines listed in §7.7; B5 is ours, B6 is the proposal-mandated
  comparison.
